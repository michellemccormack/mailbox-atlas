Can you use a virtual mailbox for a bank account?

Usually yes as a mailing address, and usually no as your home address or your company's street address. Banks have to collect a real street address under federal rules, and a virtual mailbox is easy to spot because every one carries a "PMB" or "#" number. Here's what the rule says, what one business-banking platform publishes, and what mailbox companies tell their own customers.

What federal law asks banks to collect

Under the Customer Identification Program rule, a bank must get an address from each new customer before opening an account [31 CFR 1020.220]:

  • For a person: "a residential or business street address." Someone with no street address can give an APO/FPO box or the street address of next of kin or another contact person.
  • For a company (corporation, LLC, partnership, trust): "a principal place of business, local office, or other physical location."

The rule also has banks set "risk-based procedures" to verify identity, so each bank decides how to treat a commercial mail address [31 CFR 1020.220]. That's why answers differ from bank to bank.

Why a bank can tell it's a virtual mailbox

Virtual mailbox companies are Commercial Mail Receiving Agencies (CMRAs) under USPS rules. Each CMRA registers with its Post Office, and mail to its customers must show "PMB" (private mailbox) or "#" and a box number, for example "10 MAIN ST PMB 234" [USPS DMM 508.1.8]. So the address itself tells a bank it isn't where you live or work.

What mailbox providers tell their customers

  • US Global Mail: "Yes and No!" Most banks and card companies let customers use the address as a personal address, but "some banks have recently started asking for a personal address, which is not a CMRA" address. In that case customers give a friend's, family member's or company's address as the personal address and the US Global Mail address as the mailing address [USGM].
  • Virtual Post Mail: "A virtual mailbox won't work for banking, lease agreements, or utility bills." It sells a leased office address (TruLease, $400 to $700/mo) and a residential address (TruResidence, $300 to $600/mo) for those cases [VPM] [TruLease] [TruResidence].
  • Stable: treats its mailbox address as "a mail-forwarding address" for Mercury's legal-address field, but fine for the mailing-address field [Stable].

A setup that usually works

  1. Home or physical address: use where you actually live (or your real office). For a remote LLC, Mercury accepts a residential address as the company's physical address [Mercury].
  2. Legal address for an LLC: the address on your formation documents. A registered agent address is one option some banks accept; see virtual mailbox for an LLC for what states require, and Virtual Post Mail, which includes a free registered agent in five states [VPM].
  3. Mailing address: your virtual mailbox, if the bank allows it, so statements, cards and checks get scanned.
  4. Ask first. Banks set their own procedures under the federal rule [31 CFR 1020.220], so ask the bank which fields can hold a PMB address before you apply.
Don't paper over the address. Writing a mailbox address as "Suite" instead of "PMB" doesn't make it a street address. USPS rules say a CMRA address can't combine the building's suite number with your box number, and mail without the PMB or # can be returned to the sender [USPS DMM 508.1.8].

Receiving bank mail at a virtual mailbox: what it costs

Once the account is open, the mailbox handles statements, new cards and checks. Check deposit fees range from no handling fee at US Global Mail (you cover shipping) [USGM] to $10 per check at Earth Class Mail without its subscription [LegalZoom]; the full table is on virtual mailbox check deposit fees.
